Healthy and cancer tissue compared

Colorectal cancer.
Architecture rewritten.

Normal colorectal lining forms repeated, organized glands. Comparing that reference with adenocarcinoma shows why tissue architecture carries information a single cell cannot.

IN THIS GUIDEHealthy vs. cancerWhere it beginsScreening and polypsWhat a report addsSources

Colorectal cancer includes cancers that begin in the colon or rectum. Many are adenocarcinomas—cancers that arise from gland-forming epithelial cells in the lining.

01 / SIDE BY SIDE

The reference pattern makes disruption visible.

Normal colorectal mucosa contains evenly spaced gland-like structures. Their cells help absorb water and produce mucus. In colorectal adenocarcinoma, glands may become irregular, crowded, or poorly formed, and cancer cells may extend into tissue where they do not belong.

H&E stain emphasizes nuclei and surrounding tissue, but color intensity alone is not a diagnosis. Pathologists evaluate architecture, cell features, depth of invasion, and the relationship between tumor and surrounding tissue.

02 / WHERE IT STARTS

Colon and rectal cancers share a category, not one identical story.

The colon and rectum are connected parts of the large intestine, so their cancers are often discussed together. Exact location still matters for evaluation and treatment planning. The original site also keeps its name if cancer later spreads to another organ.

ORIGIN MATTERS

Cancer in the liver that began in the colon remains metastatic colorectal cancer—not a new liver cancer.

03 / BEFORE A DIAGNOSIS

Screening looks for warning signs before symptoms.

Many colorectal cancers begin in abnormal growths called polyps in the inner lining. Screening can find some polyps or signs that need further evaluation. Screening is not the same as diagnosis: an abnormal result starts a process that may include colonoscopy, removal, and pathology.

Screening choices depend on age, risk, health history, and current guidance. Cellwear does not provide personal screening recommendations; use the screening and symptoms guide to understand the difference between general education and individual care.

04 / THE COMPLETE RECORD

Grade, stage, margins, and biomarkers answer different questions.

Grade describes how the tumor looks compared with the tissue of origin. Stage describes its extent in the body. A surgical pathology report may also describe depth of invasion, lymph nodes, and margins. Biomarker tests—including tests of mismatch-repair proteins or microsatellite instability in appropriate cases—can add information that is not visible on a routine slide.
